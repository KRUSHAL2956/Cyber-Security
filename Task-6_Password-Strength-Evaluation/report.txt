SUMMARY

I created multiple passwords from weak → strong and evaluated them using an online
password strength checker (e.g., passwordmeter.com). I compared length vs. character
diversity, estimated entropy for a few samples, and summarized best practices. The
main finding: length (14–16+ chars) plus unpredictability matters most; symbols help,
but predictable patterns still score poorly.

================================================================================

SCOPE & METHOD

Tools used: Free online strength checker (no paid tools)
Samples tested: 12 passwords (8–28+ chars, various patterns)
Criteria compared: length, character sets, presence of patterns, entropy estimate

Steps I followed:
1) Drafted password candidates with increasing complexity
2) Checked each on a strength meter; noted score + feedback
3) Estimated entropy for representative samples
4) Analyzed trends and wrote final recommendations

ENTROPY ESTIMATES (ROUGH)

Formula (approx): H ≈ L × log2(N)
- N ~ 26 (lower), 52 (lower+upper), 62 (+digits), ~90 (+symbols)

Examples:
- Nvy3r6p9 → L=8, N≈36 → H≈8×log2(36)≈8×5.17≈41 bits (weak)
- longerRandom123 → L=15, N≈62 → H≈15×5.95≈89 bits (good)
- 7&Ztq4k@bP1!xF2w → L=17, N≈90 → H≈17×6.49≈110 bits (very strong)
- Carrot-River-Lime-Robot! → L=25, N≈53 (case+symbol, words) → H≈25×5.72≈143 bits (excellent)

Note: Real entropy depends on actual randomness; dictionary words reduce effective N
if words are chosen from a small known list. Use diceware/random selection to keep
wordlists large and choices unpredictable.

================================================================================

ANALYSIS & FINDINGS

1) Length vs Symbols: Adding 1–2 symbols helps a little, but doubling length helps a lot.
2) Patterns Hurt: Season+Year, Name@123, or leetspeak substitutions score poorly despite mixed sets.
3) Passphrases Win: 4–5 random words with light separators are strong and memorable.
4) Manager‑Style Random: 16–20 char fully random strings are excellent (use a password manager).
5) Uniqueness is Critical: Reuse converts one breach into many account takeovers.

================================================================================

COMMON ATTACKS (SHORT NOTES)

- Brute force: tries all combinations → mitigated by long passwords + rate limiting
- Dictionary: tries common words/phrases → mitigated by randomness/passphrases
- Credential stuffing: reuses leaked credentials → mitigated by unique passwords per site
- Phishing/social engineering: steals passwords directly → mitigated by MFA + awareness

================================================================================

BEST PRACTICES (FINAL CHECKLIST)

- Aim for 14–16+ characters; prefer longer when possible
- Use a password manager to generate and store random passwords
- Use passphrases for master passwords (4–5 random words)
- Avoid predictable patterns (Season/Year, Qwerty, Name@123)
- Turn on MFA wherever available (TOTP, security keys)
- Never reuse passwords; rotate affected accounts after breaches

================================================================================

INTERVIEW PREP — SHORT ANSWERS

Q: What makes a password strong?  
A: Length, unpredictability, and diversity of characters (or random passphrases), plus uniqueness per account.

Q: Common password attacks?  
A: Brute force, dictionary, credential stuffing, phishing/social engineering.

Q: Why is length important?  
A: It grows the search space exponentially; longer passwords make brute force infeasible.

Q: What is a dictionary attack?  
A: An attacker tries words/phrases from large lists of common passwords and patterns.

Q: What is multi‑factor authentication (MFA)?  
A: An extra verification step (e.g., TOTP or security key) that protects accounts even if passwords leak.

Q: How do password managers help?  
A: They generate and store unique, long random passwords so users don’t reuse or memorize weak ones.

Q: What are passphrases?  
A: A sequence of random words; long and memorable with high effective entropy.

Q: Common mistakes in password creation?  
A: Short length, reuse, predictable patterns (Season+Year), simple substitutions (P@ssw0rd!).

================================================================================

CONCLUSION

I evaluated multiple passwords and confirmed that length and randomness dominate strength.
Passphrases and manager‑generated passwords are practical ways to achieve high entropy.
With MFA and non‑reuse, overall account security improves significantly.
